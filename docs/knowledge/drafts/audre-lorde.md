# Audre Lorde

Candidate English draft · not approved or published.

Poet, essayist and Black lesbian feminist who made difference, desire and the practice of speaking central to her writing and organising.


## Poetry as a way of knowing

Audre Lorde was born in New York in 1934 and worked as a librarian before becoming widely known as a poet and teacher. Her writing brings bodily experience and public life into the same frame. Love, anger, illness and the demand to be heard are not treated as distractions from politics; they help explain what politics asks of a person.


## Works to begin with

The poetry collections Coal and The Black Unicorn offer ways into Lorde’s language and recurring concerns. The Cancer Journals approaches illness through her experience of breast cancer and mastectomy. Zami: A New Spelling of My Name combines the telling of a life with the women and relationships that shaped it. These books show why reducing her to a famous quotation misses the range of her work.


## Difference without erasure

Lorde challenged movements that asked people to put aside some parts of themselves in order to join. Her essays ask what happens when feminist spaces ignore racism, or when a politics of racial solidarity leaves sexuality unspoken. The point is not that everyone’s experience is the same. It is that an alliance becomes weaker when it cannot acknowledge the differences within it.


## Making places for other voices

Publishing was part of that work. Lorde helped establish Kitchen Table: Women of Color Press, creating space for writers whose work was poorly served by dominant publishing institutions. Her teaching and organising likewise connected ideas to the practical question of who gets to speak and circulate their work.


## Reading her in context

Lorde died in 1992. Her writing continues to be read across poetry, feminism and queer thought, but it rewards attention to its particular settings and arguments. Read an essay alongside the circumstances it addresses, and a poem as more than a summary of a political position. The clarity of a line should invite a closer reading of the work around it.
